A man with a life’s work, and three questions

America’s School Trust Library is an Oregon nonprofit public benefit corporation, Oregon Registry No. 259005891, incorporated July 21, 2026. It keeps the documentary record of America’s school trust lands — the land Congress granted each new state to support its public schools, and the permanent funds that land feeds. Much of that record was never in an archive: it sits in basements, filing cabinets and aging websites, and when the person who kept it dies, most of it goes too. What follows is one collection arriving, dated so it can be checked, and it includes the parts that went badly.

Bob Zybach is a historian — Oregon forest history, oral history and historical ecology. Between 1975 and 1995, twenty-one people who had lived their lives in Soap Creek Valley, north of Corvallis, told its history into a tape recorder; he edited the interviews into fifteen indexed books, printed between 1990 and 2009, most by Oregon State University’s Research Forests and College of Forestry. Later he did the same on the Elliott State Forest, Oregon school trust land for most of a century — which is why a school-trust library keeps this record.

On the night of June 11, 2026 he was sent a packet: an invitation to help found the Library, with a draft collection policy and a form of gift whose terms were already a licence rather than a sale. He answered the next evening with concerns rather than thanks.

First, where will it be housed? Can OSU Archives … be approached as a key repository? People will be uncomfortable working on something that is largely conceptual, focused on a topic that few folks know or care about, and being formed at a location that could disappear at any given time.

Second is compensation. I’m not sure I’m comfortable gifting several decades of my work … What would be the value to me?

Quotation pending his written approval — Bob Zybach, email, June 12, 2026

The third concern was his time. Nothing in the packet answered any of the three. What changed was the paper: Donor became Contributor, the option under which a creator assigns copyright to the Library was struck out, the originals would stay wherever he chose, and everything would be reversible.

On August 27 and 28 the Library built a first edition from what he sent. All fifteen books were made page-addressable — 1,591 printed pages, each keeping its printed number as its permanent address, so a citation made in 1994 still lands. His own back-of-book index came back live: 1,823 headings, 12,618 references, a page for every place a narrator named. No sentence of his text was rewritten; where tape and page disagreed, the item went into a queue for him and nowhere else.

Things I was trying to do in the 1990s so far as publications and interactive maps would have cost somewhere between $30,000 and $90,000+ and been lesser quality than this! … Many hours spent on these tasks have now been proven worthwhile — 30 years later!

This “first test” is wonderful and even surprising in quality, speed, and accountability!

Then the Library found out it was wrong

Before anything went public, the Library checked who owned what against the primary record rather than its own assumptions — finding aids, which are the descriptions archives publish of what they hold; the state and federal registries; and the appendix of his 1999 thesis that prints the seven release forms used between 1975 and 1998. That research, dated August 31, 2026, corrected the Library three times.

  • The Elliott material is not under an open licence, as the Library had twice said. That licence line sits in a web page’s footer and covers the website, not the archive.
  • The books are not in the public domain. The rule forfeiting copyright for want of a notice ended in March 1989, and the nine examined are all 1990 or later; the other six are dated but unverified.
  • He was not the interviewer. The finding aid credits four interviewers and eight research assistants; for about a third of the interviews he conducted none, and holds no interviewer’s copyright at all.

A fourth error was the Library’s own: the June draft recited provisions of the Library’s articles of incorporation that the articles actually filed with the State do not contain. He had been handed a document promising something that did not exist. He was told so in writing, and the promise was written into the draft licence as a covenant, where it will bind once the licence is signed.

The collection went public on September 4, 2026 — and 108 printed pages did not: 76 pages that locate sites, 28 that carry personal data, and 4 held at the creator’s decision. One plain notice covers them all and keeps each page’s printed number and permanent link, so a citation to a held page still lands; the reasons are not published. The Library’s ground is narrow: printing a passage in a book in 1993 and putting a searchable point on a public map in 2026 are not the same act, and the second would be the Library’s own. Consultation continues, and a hold lifts as easily as it was set. One thing more was true that day and still is: his written permission is not yet recorded, and the site says so on its own face.

That night he was sent a report written for him alone — thirty-six pages, its decisions set out as cards he could tick, cross out or argue with. He read the first seven before midnight, worked on to page 27 before dawn, and sent those pages back with twenty of them marked up.

The same email asked for three further changes, and asked whether that was the right way to ask for them. That is where the record stands: in the middle, with the creator asking for edits and getting them.

The method, plainly

  1. The material stays his. The licence is non-exclusive: permission to publish that leaves him free to publish, sell or license the same material anywhere else. He keeps his copyright and every commercial right, the originals stay where he keeps them, and the Library pays him nothing and takes nothing exclusive.

  2. Sensitive pages are held before publication, not after a complaint — one identical notice, the printed page number and permanent link preserved, the reasons kept internal.

  3. Rights are checked against the primary record — the release forms, the finding aids, the registries — including when the answer embarrasses the Library.

  4. The edition admits it is unfinished. Every page carries this: “Working edition, September 2026 — published with the creator’s participation while rights and consultation reviews continue. Some pages are held.” Beside it sit a takedown policy under which a narrator’s or a family’s objection ends the matter, and a numbered corrections log. The errata rule here is his: an error is corrected without ceremony but recorded once, in one dated log — the Library’s own ledger.

  5. A recorded conversation counts as instructions. Talking is faster than writing for a man with a full week; what he says on tape is what gets built.

  6. He gets a document he can mark up — decisions as cards, the recommendation stated as a recommendation, with room to cross it out.

  7. Anything can be undone. Holds lift, pages come down, series close, the edition can be withdrawn. That was the promise of August 21, and it is why the rest was possible.

What stays out, whatever else changes

The Library does not print, here or anywhere: anything about the creator’s finances; anything about the other organizations he serves; anything about the burial-site question beyond the fact that those pages are held; anyone else’s private words. Nothing appears under his name that he has not seen.

The same door, for anyone else

Nothing here worked only because it was this collection. A retired agency employee with thirty years of files, a county historian with a shelf of self-published volumes, a family with a grandfather’s papers — the seven rules above are the whole of the method, and they do not change with the collection. The terms are the same for everyone: your material stays yours, access is free, the mistakes are published with numbers on them, and you may stop it at any point. The Library cannot promise money. It can promise that nothing will happen to your work that you did not agree to. If you hold a record like that, write to library@schooltrusts.net, or start at schooltrusts.org/contribute/.
