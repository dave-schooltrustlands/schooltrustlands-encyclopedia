*About this appendix.* The hornbook reads forward, from doctrine to cases. This appendix reads backward, from state to doctrine: Minnesota's cases are listed in bullet form, each entry cross-citing the hornbook sections it supports, so a practitioner can open her own state's entry and walk into the treatise from there. Every case below was read against the full primary opinion held in the Library's collection; claims that could not be resolved from a primary are flagged rather than asserted.

*Why Minnesota is worth a reader's time.* Minnesota's 1857 act is the earliest enabling act in this treatise to grant both sections 16 and 36, and its supreme court said plainly what the grant made the State. In 1907 the court held that the grant "was not made to the state, in its proprietary capacity, but in trust," and that no one can take school land from the State by adverse possession. Read with the two later opinions held here, the Minnesota line also does something few state lines do: it marks where the protection stops.

- In 1914 the court let a school district take school land by condemnation at its appraised value. It held the constitution's public-sale clause satisfied: the value was appraised and paid as on a sale, and the price went into the school fund for every district in the state.
- In 1963 it held that money the State had merely credited to the school fund, from iron ore under a lake bed the State turned out not to own, had no constitutional protection from appropriation by the legislature.

On this line the constitutional protection attaches to the proceeds of the federally granted lands, not to everything that is credited to the fund.

## MINNESOTA

**Granting instruments.** Minnesota's grant came in two steps. The held opinions describe both; neither act is held in this collection.

- **The territorial reservation, 1849.** Section 18 of the Organic Act of Minnesota Territory, passed March 3, 1849, "reserved sections 16 and 36 of every township for the purpose of being applied to schools of the territory and future state." These are the court's words, not the statute's. *Murtaugh v. Chicago, Milwaukee & St. Paul Ry. Co.*, 102 Minn. 52, 54 (1907), citing "9 St. 408, c. 121".

- **The enabling act, 1857.** Section 5 of the Act of February 26, 1857, ch. 60, granted the state sections 16 and 36 in every township for the use of schools, on condition that the constitutional convention accept the grant, "and, if it were, then its terms should become obligatory on the United States and the state of Minnesota." *Murtaugh*, at 54 (the court's paraphrase of § 5, citing "11 St. 167, c. 60").

[VERIFY: the text of both acts, and the admission act of May 11, 1858, which the Library's research file cites as ch. 31, 11 Stat. 285. No primary held here quotes any of them.] → Ch. 2, § 2.4 (published as § 1.4); § 2.7 (accepted compacts).

**Constitutional reception.** The held opinions cite the 1857 Minnesota Constitution by its original numbering.

- **Article 8, § 2, the public-sale clause.** *Independent School District v. State* quotes the restriction that "no portion of said lands shall be sold otherwise than at public sale," and notes that the same section makes the proceeds of the sales "or other disposition" a perpetual fund for the schools (124 Minn. at 278).

- **Acceptance.** *Murtaugh* cites article 2, § 3 and article 8, § 2 for the convention's and the people's acceptance of the grant, and for the provisions that the proceeds remain a perpetual school fund and that no portion of the lands be sold otherwise than at public sale (102 Minn. at 54–55).

- **What article 8, § 2 protects.** *Youngstown Mines Corp. v. Prout* reads it as protecting from legislative appropriation only the proceeds of lands granted by the United States for the use of schools (266 Minn. at 479).

[VERIFY: the constitutional text itself, original and current. The Library's state page cites the current fund provision as article XI, § 8; no primary held here quotes it.] → § 3.2; § 3.3.

**Administering bodies.** In the held opinions the state auditor sold school lands: "No greater rights can be acquired in the condemnation proceeding than under a sale by the state auditor." (124 Minn. at 277–78.) In 1963 the commissioner of conservation passed on claims for refund of money paid into the treasury under Minn. St. 6.136 (266 Minn. at 455, 470–71). **Why Minnesota matters to the field.** Four contributions.

1. **Trust character as consideration and covenant.** *Murtaugh* reads the grant and its acceptance as a bargain. The grant was made "in trust"; the trust "was the substantial consideration for the grant which induced the United States to make it"; and the State "by its constitution solemnly covenanted with the United States" to apply the lands to the sole use of its schools (102 Minn. at 55). This is one of the clearest state-court statements of the compact reading examined in Chapter 2. → § 2.7; § 4.4.

2. **No adverse possession against the school lands.** *Murtaugh* holds that title to the granted school lands "cannot be acquired by adverse possession, as against the state" (at 56). It reaches that result by construing Minnesota's own limitations statute in light of the grant and the constitution. Compare *Alabama v. Schmidt* (1914), discussed at § 2.3, where Alabama's limitations statute ran against Alabama's own school section. → § 2.3; § 4.4.

3. **Condemnation by a school district satisfies the public-sale clause.** *Independent School District v. State* (1914) holds that a disposal in condemnation, "where the value thereof is appraised and paid substantially as in the case of a public sale, is equivalent to a sale of that character and answers every purpose of the Constitution" (124 Minn. at 277). This is the contrary answer, under a different instrument, to the Arizona condemnation bar discussed at § 10.4. → § 9.4; § 10.4.

4. **The limit of the constitutional fund.** *Youngstown Mines* (1963) holds that crediting money to the school fund does not make it trust property. Money directed into the fund from sources other than the federally granted lands has "no constitutional protection from appropriation by the legislature" (266 Minn. at 479). → § 3.3; § 5.1; § 8.2.

### The cases

- **Murtaugh v. Chicago, Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway Co.**, 102 Minn. 52, 112 N.W. 860 (1907) (Start, C.J.).
  - *Posture.* An action in ejectment in Dakota County for a strip one hundred feet wide through lot 6, section 36, township 115, range 17.
    - Lot 6 "was a part of the grant of lands to the state of Minnesota for the use of its schools" (at 53). The State patented the lot to the plaintiff on May 31, 1904.
    - The railway had held the strip as part of its right of way "under color of title twenty five years prior to the commencement of this action" (at 53), and claimed title by adverse possession.
    - The trial court directed a verdict for the railway. The plaintiff appealed from the denial of a new trial (at 52–53).
  - *The question.* "The sole question which the facts stated raise is whether, in view of the character of the title of the state to its school lands, title thereto can be acquired by adverse possession." (At 53.) The railway relied on the statute making limitations apply to the State: "Such limitation shall apply to actions by or in behalf of the state and the several political divisions thereof" (R. L. 1905, § 4072, quoted at 54).
  - *Holdings.*
    1. The character of the State's title. "Our school land grant, then, was not made to the state, in its proprietary capacity, but in trust, for the explicit purpose of having the lands applied to the use of the schools of the state. This was the substantial consideration for the grant which induced the United States to make it. The state accepted the trust, and by its constitution solemnly covenanted with the United States to apply the granted lands to the sole use of its schools according to the purpose of the grant, and prohibited the sale of any portion of the granted land except at public sale." (At 55.)
    2. The statute construed. Given that title, "it is unthinkable that the legislature intended" the limitations statute "to provide a way whereby the trust as to any of the school lands might be defeated, and title thereto acquired by adverse possession, contrary to the mandate of the constitution that title thereto could only be obtained by a public sale thereof" (at 55). The court added that if the statute had to be read to allow it, the statute would violate both the terms of the grant and the state constitution (at 55–56). But "the statute fairly may be given a construction which is consistent with the terms of the school land grant", and on that reading "the legislature did not intend to provide for the acquisition of the title to school lands by adverse possession" (at 56).
    3. The rule. Title to lands granted to the State by the United States for the use of its schools "cannot be acquired by adverse possession, as against the state" (at 56). "Order reversed and a new trial granted." (At 56.)
  - *The federal analogy.* The court relied on *Northern Pacific Ry. Co. v. Townsend*, 190 U.S. 267, which held that adverse possession under a state statute could not defeat a federal railroad right of way. It quoted the Supreme Court: to give a state limitations statute that effect "would be to allow that to be done by indirection which could not be done directly" (at 55).
  - ⚠ *Cite with care.*
    - The holding is one of statutory construction. The statement that the statute would otherwise violate the grant and the constitution is conditional, and the court did not need to rest on it.
    - Seven years later *Alabama v. Schmidt*, 232 U.S. 168, 173 (1914), declined to apply *Townsend*'s rule to school lands, saying it "does not apply to a gift to a State for a public purpose of which that State is the sole guardian and minister." *Murtaugh*'s result rests on Minnesota's own statute, grant and constitution. Do not cite it for a federal rule against adverse possession of school lands.
    - The case protects the State's title against a private claimant. It says nothing about the duties of the officers who manage the lands.
    - In the held copy, the three-sentence passage quoted in holding 1 ends with a closing quotation mark that has no opening mark. [VERIFY against the printed reporter whether the court was quoting an earlier source; if so, cite the passage as quoted in *Murtaugh*.]
  - → § 2.3; § 2.7; § 4.4.

- **Independent School District of the City of Virginia v. State**, 124 Minn. 271, 144 N.W. 960 (1914) (Brown, C.J.).
  - *Posture.* Proceedings under chapter 258, Laws 1913, to condemn a tract of state school land for the use of the school district of Virginia, Minnesota, "for instruction and experimentation in agriculture."
    - The attorney general moved to dismiss. He argued that school lands are not subject to condemnation, and that the constitution allows them to be sold only at public sale.
    - The motion was denied and the State appealed. "Order affirmed." (At 279.)
  - *Holdings.*
    1. **Authority to condemn state land.** "The generally accepted doctrine of practically all of the courts is that the public lands of the state may be taken under the power of eminent domain only when authority to do so is expressly or by necessary implication granted by the legislature." (At 274.) A statute of 1872 required notice to the State and an award of damages to it where an enterprise crossed "school or University lands". From that the court held "the right of condemnation of state land was granted by that statute by necessary implication. We so hold." (At 275.) The 1905 revision carried the implied right forward "to all condemnation proceedings" (at 276).
    2. **The two capacities, raised and set aside.** "Though there is force in the suggestion of the attorney general that a distinction might well be made between land held by the state in its proprietory capacity, and that held in trust for educational purposes, the distinction was not made in the University case, and we are not disposed to change the rule of that decision, since for many years the legislature has permitted it to stand as an expression of the law of the state upon the subject." (At 276–77; "proprietory" is the source's spelling.)
    3. **The public-sale clause.** The court called this "the most serious question in the case" (at 277). It held that "a disposal of state lands in condemnation proceedings, where the value thereof is appraised and paid substantially as in the case of a public sale, is equivalent to a sale of that character and answers every purpose of the Constitution" (at 277). Its reasons:
       - "No greater rights can be acquired in the condemnation proceeding than under a sale by the state auditor." (At 277–78.) "All mineral rights must be reserved to the state" (at 278).
       - "In the case at bar the land in question is being taken for educational purposes, the purpose for which it was granted to the state by the Federal government." (At 278.)
       - The district must pay the value fixed by the condemnation commissioners, or by the court on appeal, and the amount becomes part of the general school fund. "The result of which is that no special privilege is acquired or gained by this particular school district over other districts." (At 278.)
    4. **The purpose of the clause.** "[T]he intention was to surround the disposal of the lands with publicity, thus avoiding private or secret dealings, which often result disastrously to the best interests of the state." (At 278.) The same section speaks of proceeds of sales "or other disposition", which shows the framers contemplated disposal otherwise than at auction. "[S]o long as the disposal is attended with publicity and in conformity with the requirements of the law in respect to the particular proceedings in which the title is sought to be acquired, the spirit, if not the letter, of the Constitution is complied with and not violated." (At 278.)
    5. **Sister states.** The court found the same view in Washington and Idaho, citing *Hollister v. State*, 9 Idaho 8, and *Roberts v. Seattle*, 63 Wash. 573. It quoted the Washington opinion's reason, "that condemnation had all the elements of a public sale" (at 279).
  - ⚠ *Cite with care.*
    - The measure is appraised value, fixed by commissioners or by a court (at 278), not a price tested at auction. That is the point on which Arizona's *Deer Valley* reasons the other way (§ 10.4). The two courts construed different instruments: Minnesota's constitutional public-sale clause, and Arizona's 1910 enabling act and constitution.
    - The court's premise is *University of Minnesota v. St. Paul & N. P. Ry. Co.*, 36 Minn. 447, which "differentiated between state lands in actual use and those not in use and subject to sale" (at 277). The rule reaches land of the second kind.
    - The public-sale holding is stated generally (at 277). The court also relied on two facts of this case: the taking was for the educational purpose of the grant, and the price went to the fund for all districts (at 278).
    - The held copy carries scanning errors and a one-character length discrepancy. [VERIFY: quotations against the printed reporter; the decision date, which the held copy does not carry (the Library's catalog record gives January 9, 1914).]
  - → § 3.4; § 5.1; § 9.4; § 10.4.

- **Youngstown Mines Corp. v. Prout**, 266 Minn. 450, 124 N.W.2d 328 (1963) (Nelson, J.; Sheran, J., took no part).
  - *What it is.* Certiorari review of the commissioner of conservation's refusal to refund royalties paid to the State under a 1924 fifty-year lease of part of the bed of Rabbit Lake, in Crow Wing County, for mining iron ore (at 455).
    - The lease was made under Laws 1917, chapter 110. Section 2 of that act provided: "The principal of all funds arising from the disposal of such iron ore shall forever be preserved inviolate and undiminished and shall be added to the permanent school fund of the state to be invested and re-invested as provided by law" (quoted at 455).
    - The royalties were credited to the school fund.
    - The judgment in *State v. Adams*, one of the State's own quiet-title actions, adjudged that the State "has no right, title, estate, or interest in the bed of Rabbit Lake" (at 465, quoting the *Adams* judgment). The lake was "not then navigable under the Federal tests", and fee title to the lake bed is held by the riparian owners (at 474).
  - *This was not school-grant land.* The opinion never mentions sections 16 and 36, the enabling act, or any federal school grant. The ore lay under a lake the State had claimed as navigable, and the money reached the school fund only because a statute directed it there.
  - *Holdings.*
    1. **Title.** *State v. Adams* "is res judicata against the state on the question of title" (at 462).
    2. **Capacity.** "Clearly the state in entering into the lease with Youngstown acted not in its sovereign, but in its proprietary, capacity, subjecting itself to the same liability as other litigants." (At 473–74.) The State's right to royalties "was conditioned upon its ownership of the lakebed" (at 474). "The state received Youngstown’s money. It became enriched by that amount without parting with any consideration therefor." (At 475.)
    3. **The school fund.** The 1917 act "was expressly repealed by L. 1943, c. 208"; the 1943 act "contained no provision making funds received from such sources inviolate" (at 478). "Thus, it must be clear that if the funds had been impressed with an express trust by the act of 1917, that trust was revoked by the act of 1943." (At 478.) "The mere crediting of the royalties to the school trust fund did not impress them with a trust." (At 479.)
    4. **The constitutional perimeter.** Article 8, § 2 protects from legislative appropriation only the proceeds of lands granted by the United States for the use of schools. "What other moneys may be directed to be paid into the school trust fund by the legislature or by state officials have no constitutional protection from appropriation by the legislature and occupy the same status as any other fund belonging to the state and not specifically restricted by the constitution." (At 479.) "L. 1917, c. 110, is not authority for the leasing of lands the state did not own nor for retention of payments under such a lease." (At 480.)
    5. **The refund statute.** Minn. St. 6.136 allows refund of money paid "through error" or in circumstances where the State is not legally entitled to retain it. The court read the two branches as alternatives. Assuming the first branch covers payments made by factual mistake, it said that "it must be conceded that the royalty payments were made knowingly, pursuant to a lease and a judgment and in conformity with them." It allowed recovery on the second branch (at 489).
    6. **Disposition.** "Youngstown is entitled to refundment of the royalties paid under the lease" (at 490). "The determination of the court below is hereby affirmed and the within matter remanded for further proceedings in accordance with the decision herein." (At 490.)
  - ⚠ *Cite with care.*
    - This is not a school-trust-lands case. The State never owned the land.
    - The case *limits* the inviolability doctrine for money that is not the proceeds of granted lands. It does not enforce it. The court never construed "undiminished"; it held that any trust the 1917 act created had been revoked in 1943.
    - Do not describe the payments as mistaken. The court said they were made knowingly (at 489).
    - The court fixed no refund amount. The figure of $559,789.55 at 470 comes from a table in Youngstown's brief of payments to the State before *Adams*.
    - A 1944 attorney general's opinion is quoted in footnote 4 (at 489–90) as part of the record. It is not the court's holding.
    - Two cases are easily confused. *Petraborg v. Zontelli*, 217 Minn. 536 (1944), was a private injunction suit over draining the lake, and it did not decide ownership (at 456–57). *State v. Zontelli*, a Crow Wing County district-court action, reformed the lease on the parties' stipulation.
    - The opinion gives the date of Youngstown's refund claim as February 20, 1958 in its opening paragraph and as February 20, 1959 at 462. [VERIFY which is right against the reporter.] [VERIFY: the decision date, which the held copy does not carry; the Library's catalog record gives October 18, 1963.]
  - → § 3.3; § 5.1; § 8.2.

### What the build found

**1. The two-section generation began before Oregon.** The treatise's chapter on the granting instruments headed its account of the doubled grant "1859–1906" and opened with Oregon. *Murtaugh* describes the 1857 act granting both sections to Minnesota. The heading now reads "1857–1906", and a Minnesota entry opens the section. The correction is published with this appendix.

**2. *Youngstown Mines* was catalogued as authority for the rule it limits.** The Library's internal catalog described the case as turning on the 1917 act's "inviolate and undiminished" language, and as "[d]irectly relevant" to the permanent-fund-corpus doctrine. The court decided it on the lease, the stipulated judgment and the refund statute. It noted that the 1917 act had been repealed, and it held that crediting money to the fund did not make it trust property. The case is authority for the edge of the doctrine, not its core.

**3. Words were put in quotation marks that *Murtaugh* does not contain.** The internal catalog quoted the court as saying that a contrary reading "would violate" the grant and the constitution. The opinion says the statute, if it had to be read that way, "violates in this respect" both. That statement is conditional, on a reading the court rejected.
