# School Trust Lands: The Law of America's Educational Land Trusts
## Appendix A — Oklahoma (v2)

**September 6, 2026 (case line built against the three full primary opinions held in the Library's collection; first publication)**
**Drafted with Claude, an AI system, and published by America's School Trust Library. Not legal advice.**

*About this appendix.* The hornbook reads forward, from doctrine to cases. This appendix reads backward, from state to doctrine: Oklahoma's school-trust cases are listed in bullet form, each entry cross-citing the hornbook sections it supports, so a practitioner can open her own state's entry and walk into the treatise from there. Every case below was read against the full primary opinion held in the Library's collection; claims that could not be resolved from a primary are flagged rather than asserted.

*Why Oklahoma is worth a reader's time.* Two things make it unusual. Congress could not grant school sections across the eastern half of the future state, because the land had already passed to citizens of the Five Tribes under the allotment agreements — so it wrote a check instead, the only cash school endowment of its kind. And Oklahoma's courts, working from a constitution that calls the lands a "sacred trust," produced one of the country's clearer answers to a question that recurs everywhere: what happens when a legislature tells the trustee to charge less than the land is worth, in order to help somebody the legislature would like to help.

*Why this entry appears now, and what it withdraws.* Oklahoma was one of the states whose primary opinions the Library held but whose appendix had never been published. Building it disclosed that the Library's own **live** pages had for months described *OEA v. Nigh* as a revenue-diversion case in which the court enjoined the diversion and ordered the money restored. The opinion is not a diversion case, no injunction issued, and nothing was restored. It also disclosed that an anecdote about a trial judge, carried in the Library's internal authorities register and published in its catalog, is not in the opinion and could not be — the case was an original proceeding with no trial. What the build found is set out at the end. The corrections are in this pass; the record of them is in the corrections log.

## OKLAHOMA

**Granting instrument.** Oklahoma Enabling Act of June 16, 1906, 34 Stat. 267 — sections 16 and 36 of every township in the former Oklahoma Territory, granted for the use and benefit of the common schools, with cash payments in lieu of sections in the former Indian Territory, where allotment under prior federal action had foreclosed grants in place. The in-lieu cash component makes Oklahoma the instructive instrument on monetized grants. The federal text on the section grant is comparatively thin: it lacks the express *in trust*, *breach of trust* and *null and void* language that the 1910 New Mexico–Arizona Act would carry four years later. Oklahoma's own constitution supplies what the federal text does not. → § 2.4; Appendix B.

⚠ *A citation trap in the opinion itself.* *Nigh*'s disposition paragraph miscites the Enabling Act as "Act of Congress June 16, 1906, 14 U.S. St. at Large, pp. 267-279." The opinion's own opening paragraph and its footnote 2 give the correct "34 U.S. St. at Large, pp. 267-278." The error is in the published opinion, not in the Library's copy. Cite 34 Stat. 267.

**Constitutional reception and administering body.** Oklahoma was admitted November 16, 1907. Article XI, section 1 accepts the federal grants upon the conditions on which they were made and pledges the faith of the State to hold the lands and monies "as a sacred trust" — the phrase the Supreme Court of Oklahoma later held to carry the whole law of trusts into the state's organic law. The trustee body is the Commissioners of the Land Office, seated ex officio by article VI, section 32 in the Governor, Lieutenant Governor, State Auditor and Inspector, Superintendent of Public Instruction, and President of the State Board of Agriculture. The presence of the Superintendent of Public Instruction puts a beneficiary's fiduciary inside the trustee circle, which is rare among the public-land states. [VERIFY: current permanent-fund corpus, annual distribution and remaining trust acreage are carried from the Library's state dossier and are not established by any primary held here.] → Ch. 3.

**Why Oklahoma matters to the field.** *Nigh* (1982) is one of the country's strongest statements of the irrevocable-compact doctrine — that the arrangement binds successive legislatures — and *Kanaly* in South Dakota cites it for exactly that. But the deeper Oklahoma contribution is the pair of rules the three cases produce together: money that comes out of the ground is corpus, not income (*Okfuskee*, 1933); the federal trust conditions cannot be undone even by the people amending their own constitution (*Williamson*, 1956); and the trust may not be made to carry a subsidy the state wishes to grant (*Nigh*, 1982). The third is the one most often needed and most often misdescribed.

### The cases

- **Oklahoma Education Association, Inc. v. Nigh**, 642 P.2d 230 (Okla. 1982).
  - *Posture.* An original proceeding in the Supreme Court of Oklahoma. The court assumed jurisdiction "for the sole purpose of considering the constitutionality" of five statutes governing the Commissioners of the Land Office: two capping the annual rent on preference-right leases, two capping the interest chargeable on farm loans and on the unpaid purchase price of trust land, and one giving a sitting lessee a new lease without requiring him to match or exceed competing bids. The petitioner was the Oklahoma Education Association, Inc.; the Attorney General of Oklahoma intervened on the petitioner's side; school districts and groups of private citizens intervened as well. The named respondents — Governor George Nigh among them — were sued **as Commissioners of the Land Office**, the body that manages the school lands ex officio, not as general state officers.
  - *Holdings.* Barnes, V.C.J. All five statutes held to violate article XI, sections 1, 2 and 5 of the Oklahoma Constitution and the Enabling Act. The acceptance provisions "constitute an irrevocable compact between the United States and Oklahoma, for the benefit of the common schools, which cannot be altered or abrogated," 642 P.2d at 235 — the sentence *Kanaly* cites. "The express designation of the school lands and funds as a 'sacred trust' has the effect of irrevocably incorporating into the Enabling Act, Oklahoma Constitution, and conditions of the grant, all of the rules of law and duties governing the administration of trusts," *id.* at 235–36. "No Act of the Legislature can validly alter, modify or diminish the State's duty as Trustee of the school land trust to administer it in a manner most beneficial to the trust estate and in a manner which obtains the maximum benefit in return from the use of trust property or loan of trust funds," *id.* at 236. And the sentence that states the vice plainly: "Just as a State may not use school land trust funds assets to subsidize its highway construction program, a State may not use school land trust assets to subsidize farming and ranching," *id.* at 236. (The reporter's "trust funds assets" is as published.)
  - *The affirmative half of the legislative-power rule*, which is the more useful half and is rarely quoted: "While the Legislature does have the power to, and may, regulate the activities of the Commissioners, it can neither abridge nor impair their freedom to function in utmost good faith in the day-to-day discharge of their public obligation as managers of the trust estate and while acting on behalf of the State as Trustee," *id.* at 238. The rule-making power "was intended to promote rather than impede attainment of the manifest objective of the Enabling Act provisions," *id.*
  - *The calibrated-preference rule.* Footnote 10 draws the line the treatise's leasing chapter needs: the court is "not saying that under proper circumstances a preference right to re-lease trust lands requiring the current lessee to equal or exceed other bids cannot be given," only that a preference "that does not require payment of a competitive rate for such lease is violative of the trustee's duty to obtain the maximum benefit and return to the trust estate." (The reporter prints "to release" for "to re-lease" at the first occurrence.)
  - *Disposition — and the limit on it.* **No injunction issued and nothing was ordered restored.** The court directed the Commissioners to exercise their powers so as to maintain the maximum return to the trust, but because they and the citizens who had dealt with them had relied in good faith on statutes then presumed valid, it held that they "are hereby protected from any liability for any retrospective acts committed under said unconstitutional statutes" and that the decision would "operate prospectively from the date this opinion becomes final," *id.* at 239. On retroactivity, *Nigh* is the opposite of Nebraska's *Propst*. Any use of *Nigh* in a survey of statutes void from enactment has to say so.
  - *Composition.* Simms, J., dissented, joined by Doolin and Hargrave, JJ.; Opala, J., concurred in part and dissented in part. Three dissents and a partial. *Nigh* is a strong opinion but not a unanimous one, and a brief leaning hard on it should expect the point.
  - *Supports:* Ch. 4 (§§ 4.3 constitutional incorporation, 4.6(a) breaching acts invalid — subject to the prospectivity limit, 4.7 limits of legislative power); Ch. 7 (maximum benefit; prudence and productivity); Ch. 11 (preference rights and below-market renewals — the case's own subject matter).

- **State ex rel. Williamson v. Commissioners of the Land Office**, 301 P.2d 655 (Okla. 1956).
  - *Posture.* An original proceeding for an injunction against the Commissioners of the Land Office, to stop them depositing oil-and-gas lease revenues into the State Public Common School Building Equalization Fund as newly required by article 10, section 32 of the Oklahoma Constitution, adopted by the people on April 5, 1955, and by section 3 of House Bill 964 of the Twenty-fifth Legislature. The Commissioners answered that they were under a mandatory statutory duty and were holding the funds pending the court's direction. The Oklahoma Education Association appeared as amicus — arguing *against* the Enabling Act restrictions, on the ground that they lacked validity after statehood.
  - *Holdings.* Welch, J., for a unanimous court. Both the constitutional amendment and the statute are invalid. Congress "clearly expressed the intendment that proceeds derived from oil and 'gas leases on the granted lands should be held by the State as permanent trust funds, with only the interest or income derived from investments of the trust funds to ever be spent," 301 P.2d at 658. A deposit of such monies "to a fund to be expended as current revenues of the State . . . will violate the terms of the Enabling Act, an Act of Congress," *id.* And the holding sentence: the Enabling Act's regulations touching the granted lands and their proceeds "exist as valid laws of the United States, which may not be modified, restricted or changed by an Act of the Oklahoma Legislature or the people of Oklahoma in amendment of the Oklahoma Constitution," *id.* at 659. That is the strongest form of the proposition anywhere in this collection, because it binds the electorate and not merely the legislature.
  - *The equal-footing answer.* Meeting the amicus's *Coyle v. Smith* argument, the court distinguished conditions on political sovereignty from conditions on granted property: "We do not perceive a limitation or restriction on the State in the exercise of its sovereign powers in the advancement of education or of schools in the terms of Enabling Act. We see therein only regulations touching the care and disposition of properties granted in trust to the State by the Federal Government," *id.* Congressional regulations of that kind survive admission, *id.* (citing *United States v. Sandoval*, 231 U.S. 28). The court did not reject the equal-footing doctrine; it held these conditions to be a different category. That distinction is the whole of the answer and should be stated as a category distinction, not as a rejection.
  - *Remedy.* The decree both enjoins and directs: the Commissioners are "ordered, directed and enjoined from acting pursuant to" the invalidated amendment and statute, *id.*, and are directed to deposit the monies into the permanent funds instead. It is a worked example of the remedy, not merely of the right.
  - ⚠ *What this case is not.* The court says nothing about standing, capacity, or the Attorney General's authority to sue, and the Library's copy contains **no caption and no counsel list** — it begins mid-argument. The relator's identity is therefore not established by the opinion text. Cite the posture — an officer of the State suing the State's own land commissioners in the State's highest court, and winning — and never a holding on who may sue.
  - *Supports:* Ch. 4 (limits of legislative power, and of the amending power); Ch. 11 (diversion of lease and royalty revenue); Ch. 18 (remedies — injunction plus affirmative direction to the trustee); the reception and bilateral-compact line.

- **School District No. 23 of Okfuskee County v. Commissioners of the Land Office**, 166 Okla. 226, 27 P.2d 149 (1933).
  - *Posture.* Common school districts sought mandamus to compel the Commissioners of the Land Office to pay over to the districts, as current income, the bonus, delay rentals and royalty collected on oil-and-gas leases of school lands. The alternative writ was vacated and the petition denied; the districts appealed. Note the direction of the pressure — it was the *beneficiaries* who wanted the corpus spent and the *trustee* who defended it. That is worth remembering wherever the beneficiary-versus-trustee frame is applied too simply.
  - *Holdings.* Swindall, J. Affirmed; the statute sustained. "Clearly, Congress had in mind and intended that the royalty, rental, and bonus money offered for oil and gas leases, where the land was common school land, should be paid into the permanent school fund, the interest only of which should be expended in support of such schools as provided for in section 9," 166 Okla. at 229. Congress "evidently had in mind the fact that under a mineral lease there would be some depreciation of the corpus," and routed the proceeds to the permanent fund "in order that the value of lands may not be materially impaired or diminished," *id.* The operative holding and disposition arrive in one sentence: all such funds "contemplated a diminution of the corpus of the school lands and . . . the same shall be carried into and credited to the permanent funds for the uses and purposes designated in the grant of such lands by Congress to the state of Oklahoma, and the judgment of the trial court in denying the writ of mandamus should be, and the same is hereby, affirmed," *id.* at 231.
  - *The interstate line.* The court recites Wyoming's *State ex rel. School District No. 1, In Weston County, v. Snyder*, 212 P. 758, and notes that the Texas Commission of Appeals reached the same result in *State ex rel. Attorney General v. Hatcher*, 281 S.W. 192, *id.* at 230. It describes and agrees; it does not say it follows.
  - ⚠ *What this case is not.* The statute was **upheld** — this is not a legislative-limits case. Standing is never discussed. And the court does not reject the "income" label for mineral money: it cites its own *Wright v. Carter Oil Co.*, 97 Okla. 46, 223 P. 835, approvingly for the proposition that "Bonus, rentals, and royalty are income from the use of the mineral resources of the land," *id.* It is the trust structure, not the character of the money, that produces the corpus result.
  - *Supports:* Ch. 11 (corpus versus income on mineral revenue); Ch. 4 (constitutional reception — article XI, section 1's acceptance).

### What the build found

Three statements the Library had published, and one it had carried internally, did not survive contact with the opinions.

**The posture and the remedy in *Nigh* were both wrong, on five live pages.** The Library described *Nigh* as a case about "appropriation legislation" diverting trust revenues "to general state-government purposes," in which the court "permanently enjoined the diversion and ordered the revenues restored to the common school fund." None of that is in the opinion. There was no appropriation act; there were five statutes about lease rents, loan interest and re-lease preferences. No injunction issued. Nothing was restored — the court expressly protected the Commissioners and the citizens who dealt with them from liability for past acts and made its ruling prospective only. A reader who opened the opinion at page 239 could have contradicted the Library's page in a sentence. The error had propagated to the case file, the court atlas, and two Reading Room essays.

**An anecdote that could not have happened.** The Library's internal authorities register recorded, and its public catalog published, that the trial judge in *Nigh* — named as Cannon — told the parties, "Boys, the party is over." *Nigh* was an original proceeding filed in the Supreme Court of Oklahoma. There was no trial and no trial judge, and no factual record at all; the dissent says so in terms. The words appear nowhere in the opinion. The quotation is withdrawn, and this is the second consecutive state build in which a sentence attributed to a court turned out to have no source.

**"No second verified Oklahoma case is carried in the corpus."** The Library's Oklahoma entry said this from June 6, 2026 onward. *Okfuskee* and *Williamson* had both been in the collection, character-exact, since June 10. The line was false for three months and is withdrawn. Two of the three cases in this appendix were sitting unread on the Library's own shelf.

**A pinning hazard worth stating plainly.** The three Oklahoma copies do not agree about what their page numbers mean. *Nigh* carries Pacific Reporter star pages, and `642 P.2d at ___` pins are sound from 234 to 243 — though the markers for 232 and 233 appear out of order in the counsel block, and the "sacred trust" sentence straddles the 235–236 break. The *Okfuskee* copy's star pages are **Oklahoma Reports** pages, not Pacific Reporter pages, despite the parallel `27 P.2d 149` citation in its filename: `166 Okla. at ___` is verifiable and **no `27 P.2d` pin can be produced at all**. The *Williamson* copy's markers are Pacific Reporter pages, 656 through 659. Both older copies also carry optical-recognition damage inside otherwise quotable sentences — the *Okfuskee* copy's rendering of article XI, section 2 has "ancl" for "and," so the Oklahoma Constitution must be quoted from the constitution and never from that copy.
